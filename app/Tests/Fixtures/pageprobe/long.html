How Sallow Fen was drained, and what it cost

An essay by Wendell Okafor-Price · 22 minute read

A wet country

Four hundred years ago, Sallow Fen was not land in the way that we use the word today. It was a wide basin of peat, reed and open water between the chalk hills to the west and the clay ridge to the east. In winter the three small rivers that cross it, the Sallow, the Merrin and the Kell, spread out over the whole basin and joined into one shallow lake. In summer the water went back into a pattern of meres, channels and wet meadows that changed from year to year. People lived on the islands of higher ground, which local maps call "eys", and on the edges where the peat met the clay.

The people of the fen did not think of their country as waste. They cut reed for roofs and sedge for fuel. They caught eels in willow traps and sold them by the barrel in the market towns. They grazed cattle on the summer meadows and moved them to the eys when the water came back. Wildfowl gave them meat in the winter, and the meres gave them fish all year. A record from the manor of Kellby lists fourteen kinds of right that a tenant could hold in the fen, from the right to cut turf to the right to take swans' eggs on one named island.

To the landowners who lived outside the fen, this way of life looked poor and untidy. They saw thousands of acres of soil that could grow wheat if the water went away. They also saw rents that were low because most of the value of the fen was in common rights, not in fields that one person owned. This difference of view, between the people who used the wet fen and the people who wanted to own a dry one, runs through the whole story of the drainage.

The first scheme

The first serious plan to drain Sallow Fen came in 1638. A group of eleven investors, most of them from the capital, formed a company and asked the crown for the right to drain the basin. In return for the cost and the risk, they would get one third of the land that they made dry. The plan was the work of an engineer called Pieter van Loon, who had worked on drainage in the low countries across the sea.

Van Loon's idea was simple on paper. He would cut one straight channel, about twelve miles long, from the south end of the basin to the tidal river at Gullmouth. This channel, later called the Old Cut, would carry the water of the three rivers straight to the sea instead of letting it spread over the fen. Along the channel he would build high banks, so that a flood in the channel could not escape back onto the land.

The work took six years and about four thousand men. Most of them were local labourers, but the company also brought in prisoners of war as cheap workers. The diggers moved earth with spades and wheelbarrows. Horses pulled carts of clay for the banks. Records of the company show that at least 260 workers died during the work, most of them from fever in the summers of 1640 and 1641.

When the Old Cut opened in 1644, the first results were good. The winter flood of 1645 stayed in the channel, and about eleven thousand acres of the southern fen stayed dry enough for crops. The investors took their third of the land and began to let it to farmers at rents three times higher than the old ones.

The fen people fight back

For the people of the fen, the new channel was a disaster. Their common rights were in the land that was now dry, and the company did not pay them for the loss. A tenant who had grazed six cows on the summer meadows found the meadow fenced and sown with oats. The eel traps in the meres were useless because the meres were gone. Families who had lived on the eys for generations now had to pay rent for land that their grandparents had used for free.

The response began in the summer of 1646. Groups of men, and in some places women, went out at night and broke the banks of the Old Cut. They filled the new ditches, pulled down fences and drove the company's cattle off the land. The company called them rioters. In the villages they were called the Fen Watch, and many people who did not take part still gave them food and hiding places.

The fight went on for nearly twenty years. Each spring the company repaired the banks, and each winter some of them were broken again. The courts sent dozens of people to prison, but juries in the fen towns often refused to convict their neighbours. In 1653 a crowd of about three hundred people destroyed a sluice gate at Kellby in one night. The company's own accounts show that repairs of damage by the Fen Watch cost more than the original work in some years.

In the end, the Fen Watch did not win, but they did change the terms. A settlement in 1665 gave each village a block of common land inside the drained area, about one acre for each household. It was much less than the people had lost, but it gave the poorest families a place to graze a cow and cut fuel. Some of these village commons still exist, and a few are still grazed today.

The land sinks

The drainage had a problem that none of the investors expected. Peat is made mostly of water and dead plant matter. When it dries, it shrinks. When it is ploughed and exposed to the air, it also slowly rots away. Within thirty years of the opening of the Old Cut, the surface of the drained fields had dropped by more than a metre in some places.

This meant that the land was now lower than the channel that was meant to drain it. Water could no longer flow from the fields into the Old Cut by itself. Farmers found their ditches full of standing water each winter, and the fields that had grown wheat in the 1650s were wet meadows again by the 1680s. Some farmers gave up their leases, and the investors' rents fell.

The answer was to lift the water. From about 1690, landowners began to build wind pumps along the main drains. A wind pump was a small windmill that turned a scoop wheel. The wheel lifted water from a low ditch into a higher drain, from where it flowed to the Old Cut. At the peak, around 1750, there were more than seven hundred wind pumps in Sallow Fen. Old paintings show the flat land with mills standing every few hundred metres in every direction.

Wind pumps had a clear weakness: they only worked when the wind blew. A calm week in a wet winter could undo the work of a month. They also could not lift water very high, usually less than two metres. As the peat kept shrinking, farmers had to build two or even three pumps in a row, each lifting the water a little higher. The system worked, but it was fragile and expensive to keep.

Steam

The first steam pump in Sallow Fen started work in 1821 at Hollins Corner, on the east side of the basin. It replaced eleven wind pumps, and it could lift water more than three metres in any weather. A local newspaper reported that the engine drained four thousand acres in the first winter, land that had been under water for most of the previous ten years.

Within forty years, steam had replaced almost all the wind pumps. The big engine houses, with tall brick chimneys, became the new landmarks of the fen. Each engine needed coal, which came by barge along the main drains, and a team of men who lived in cottages next to the engine house. The engine man was an important person in his district. In a flood, the farms of a whole parish depended on his skill and on his supply of coal.

The steam engines made the fen into some of the most productive farmland in the country. Wheat, potatoes, sugar beet and celery grew in the black peat soil. Railway lines arrived in the 1840s and carried the crops to the cities. The old market towns on the edge of the fen grew rich, and new villages grew up along the drains and the railway lines.

But the engines also made the old problem faster. With better pumps, farmers could dry the peat deeper, and drier peat shrank faster. An iron post that a landowner drove into the ground at Hollins Corner in 1851 shows the change. The top of the post was level with the ground when it went in. Today the top of the post stands almost four metres above the field.

The great flood

By the early twentieth century, much of Sallow Fen lay below the level of the sea. The banks of the Old Cut and the other main channels held back water that was higher than the fields on both sides. Everyone in the fen knew that a break in a main bank would be serious. Few people knew how serious it could be.

The test came in March 1912. Heavy snow in February was followed by a sudden thaw and three days of rain. The three rivers rose faster than at any time in living memory. On the night of 14 March, a section of the east bank of the Old Cut gave way near the village of Marsh Ashby. Water poured through a gap that grew to more than sixty metres wide.

By the morning, about twenty-six thousand acres were under water. Marsh Ashby, Kell End and part of Hollins Corner were flooded to the height of the first floor of the houses. Twenty-three people died, most of them in the first hours, when the water came through the dark at the speed of a running man. More than two thousand people had to leave their homes, and many could not come back for more than a year.

The repair of the bank took eleven weeks. Then the pumps had to lift all the flood water back out of the basin, which took most of the summer. The engines ran day and night. Coal ran short in May, and the drainage board had to buy it at high prices from the north. The total cost of the flood, in the money of the time, was more than the board's income for twenty years.

A new kind of board

Before 1912, the drainage of Sallow Fen was in the hands of about forty small boards. Each board looked after one district, with its own pumps and its own rates. They often argued with each other. A board that pumped its water into a shared drain could cause a flood in the district downstream, and there was no one to decide who was right.

After the flood, a public inquiry found that this division of power was one of the causes of the disaster. The bank that broke at Marsh Ashby belonged to a board that had not been able to pay for its upkeep for years. Its neighbours had refused to help, because the bank did not protect their land directly. The inquiry wrote that "the safety of the whole fen depends on its weakest bank, and the weakest bank belongs to its poorest board".

In 1919 a new law joined the small boards into one body, the Sallow Fen Catchment Board. It had the power to raise a rate from every landowner in the basin and to spend the money where the risk was greatest. It also took over the main banks and channels, while the local boards kept only the small drains between fields.

The new board spent its first ten years on the main banks. It raised them by an average of one metre and widened their base. It also built the first concrete sluices at the mouth of the Old Cut, so that high tides could not push sea water up the channel. These works were not exciting, and few people noticed them. But in the flood of 1937, which was as large as the flood of 1912, no bank broke.

Diesel and electricity

The steam engines were replaced in their turn. The first diesel pumps arrived in the 1920s, and the last steam engine in the fen stopped work in 1951. Diesel pumps were smaller and needed fewer men. An engine that had needed a team of four could now be run by one man who visited it twice a day.

From the 1960s, electric pumps took over. They could be started and stopped by a float switch in the drain, so that they worked only when the water reached a set level. Today the Catchment Board runs 86 pumping stations. Most of them have no staff at all. A control room in the town of Merrinford watches the water levels on screens and can start any pump from a distance.

The old engine houses were left empty, and many were pulled down. A few were saved by local groups. The engine house at Hollins Corner is now a museum, with its steam engine restored to working order. On four weekends each year, volunteers light the boiler and run the engine for visitors. The scoop wheel turns, but the water it lifts goes round in a closed loop, because the real drainage now happens a few hundred metres away, by an electric pump the size of a car.

The peat runs out

By the end of the twentieth century, a new problem was clear. In many parts of the fen, the peat was almost gone. The soil that had made the fen rich was not a renewable resource. It had been shrinking and rotting for more than three hundred years. In the west of the basin, where the peat had been thinnest, the plough now turned up the grey clay that lay beneath it.

Clay is good soil in its own way, but it is heavy and cold, and it needs different crops and different machines. Farmers who had grown celery and potatoes on light black soil had to change their whole business. Some changed to wheat and oilseed. Others sold their land.

The loss of the peat also had effects beyond the farms. Peat holds a great deal of carbon. When it dries and rots, the carbon goes into the air. Scientists from the university at Merrinford estimated in 2009 that the drained peat of Sallow Fen still releases about the same amount of carbon each year as a town of sixty thousand people. The more the fields are drained and ploughed, the faster this happens.

There is also the matter of the level. Each centimetre that the land sinks makes the pumps work harder and makes a flood worse if a bank breaks. In the lowest parts of the fen, the fields now lie more than four metres below the level of high tide at Gullmouth. The board estimates that the cost of pumping rises by about one percent for each two centimetres of further loss.

Putting the water back

In 2004 a group of nature trusts bought a block of about 900 acres of worn-out farmland at Kell End, near the place where the bank had broken in 1912. Their plan was to do the opposite of what the drainers had done for three centuries: to raise the water level and let the land become wet again.

The project, called Kell End Wetland, closed some of the drains and turned off two pumps. Within three years, the fields were covered with shallow water in winter and wet grass in summer. Reed began to grow in the old ditches. Birds that had not nested in the fen for a hundred years came back, among them the bittern, a shy heron whose booming call was once a common sound of the fen.

The wetland also stopped the loss of peat on its own land. Measurements by the trusts show that the surface of the wet fields has been stable since 2008, while the farmland next to it has sunk by about eleven centimetres in the same time. Some areas have even begun to build new peat, very slowly, from the dead reed.

The project was not popular with everyone. Some farmers worried that the wet fields would make their own land wetter, and that the board would spend less on the pumps that protected them. The trusts answered with a ring of new banks and a promise to pay for any damage. After fifteen years, no claim has been made, but the worry has not gone away.

Farming on wet land

A newer idea tries to find a middle way between the dry fen of the drainers and the wild wetland of the nature trusts. It is called wet farming, and it means growing crops that like high water. A small number of farms in Sallow Fen now grow reed for roofs, as the fen people did four hundred years ago. Others grow a type of grass that can be cut and pressed into building boards, or a moss that gardeners can use in place of peat compost.

These crops keep the water level close to the surface, so the peat stops shrinking. They also earn money, which a nature reserve does not. The problem is that the markets for them are small. A farmer who grows potatoes can sell the crop to a supermarket the next week. A farmer who grows reed has to find a thatcher who needs it, and there are only a few dozen thatchers in the region.

The Catchment Board supports some trials of wet farming. Its interest is practical: land that stays wet does not need pumping, and a lower pumping bill is welcome. The board also points out that it cannot let one field flood without thinking about the fields next to it. Water does not stop at the edge of a farm. A wet farm needs its own banks and sluices, and these cost money to build.

The last eel men

Not every part of the old fen life ended with the Old Cut. The eel trade survived in the main channels for more than three hundred years after the meres were gone. Eels do not care much whether they live in a natural mere or a straight drain. As long as the water connected to the sea, young eels came up from the coast each spring, and the men of the fen caught the grown eels each autumn as they moved down again.

In 1900 there were still about sixty families in the basin who made most of their living from eels. They used long nets of fine mesh, set across a drain at night, and traps of willow that their fathers had taught them to weave. The eels went by cart and later by train to the cities, where they were sold live in markets or cooked in pies and jellies.

The trade fell slowly in the twentieth century. The new pumps and sluices made it harder for young eels to reach the fen from the sea. Pollution from farm chemicals made some drains almost empty of fish. And tastes changed: fewer people in the cities wanted to eat eels. When the eel man Albert Rusk of Kellby gave up in 1987, a local paper called him the last of his kind. That was not quite true, because two of his cousins carried on for a few more years, but by the end of the century the trade was gone.

Today eels are protected, because their numbers across the whole continent have fallen by more than ninety percent. The Catchment Board has fitted eel passes at several of its pumping stations. These are narrow ramps with wet brushes, which a young eel can climb around a pump or a sluice. Counts at the Gullmouth pass show a small rise in the number of young eels since 2015, but nobody yet knows if the trend will last.

The war years

The two world wars changed the fen in ways that are easy to forget. In both wars, the country needed every acre of farmland it could find, and the government gave the drainage boards money to bring wet land back into use. In the second war, a special committee took over farms that it judged badly run and gave them to new tenants. Some of the last wet meadows of the basin were ploughed for the first time in the 1940s.

The war also brought new people. Women of the land army worked on the fen farms from 1939, and many of them had never lived in the country before. Their letters, some of which are kept in the archive at Merrinford, describe the flat land, the cold wind from the east and the mud of the winter fields. One of them wrote that "the sky here is bigger than anywhere I have been, and there is nothing at all between you and it".

Prisoners of war worked in the fen too, as they had three hundred years before, during the digging of the Old Cut. This time the work was cleaning drains and lifting potatoes. Records show that relations between the prisoners and the local people were mostly good. A few of the prisoners stayed after the war, married local women and farmed in the basin for the rest of their lives.

The war left marks on the land. Concrete pillboxes still stand at many bridges over the main drains, built in 1940 when the army expected an invasion from the sea. An airfield on the clay ridge at the east edge of the basin became a farm again after 1946, but its long runways are still visible from the air as pale lines across the fields.

Measuring a moving land

Because the land of the fen keeps sinking, measuring it has always been a problem. A map of field levels made in one decade is wrong in the next. The early drainers used simple levels and long staffs, and their records are full of corrections. Different surveyors took their heights from different marks, so two maps of the same district can disagree by half a metre.

The iron post at Hollins Corner was one answer. Its builder drove it down through the peat until it rested on the firm clay below. Since the clay does not shrink, the top of the post stays at the same height while the ground around it falls. Visitors can read the loss of soil on the post like the marks on a ruler. The post is now one of the best-known sights of the fen and appears on postcards and in school books.

Modern surveys use satellites and planes with laser scanners. A survey flight can measure the height of every field in the basin to within a few centimetres in a single day. The board repeats such a survey every five years. The results confirm what the old post shows: the land still sinks, by about one centimetre a year in the lowest parts of the fen where the peat is still deep, and by much less in the higher parts where the peat is almost gone.

These numbers matter for planning. A pumping station built today must still work when the land around it is thirty centimetres lower. A bank built to a certain height protects less each year as the fields behind it sink. The engineers of the board now design every new work for a land that will be lower at the end of its life than at the start.

Villages of the dry fen

The villages that grew up on the drained land are different from the old villages on the eys. The old villages stand on small hills, with their churches at the highest point and their houses close together. The new villages, built after the drainage, lie in long lines along the roads and the drains. Each farmhouse stands in its own fields, and the nearest neighbour may be half a mile away.

Life in these places was hard and often lonely. Children walked miles to school along roads that flooded in winter. Doctors were far away. The pub, the chapel and the shop were the centres of social life, and the arrival of the bus in the 1920s was a major event. Many families left for the towns in the second half of the twentieth century, when farms grew larger and needed fewer workers.

Some of the villages are now growing again. New houses for people who work in Merrinford and the other towns have been built on the edges of several of them. Many of the new residents know little about the drainage and are surprised to learn that their gardens lie below the level of the sea. The board has started to send a short leaflet to each new household, which explains the pumps, the banks and what to do in a flood warning.

The cost, counted again

It is easy, from a distance of four centuries, to judge the drainage of Sallow Fen as a mistake. That would not be fair to the people who did it, or to the people who have lived on the dry fen since. The drained land fed millions of people over three hundred years. It gave work to farm labourers, engine men, bargemen and railway workers. Towns that would not exist without it are now home to more than two hundred thousand people.

But the cost was real, and much of it fell on people who had no voice in the decision. The fen people lost a whole way of life in the seventeenth century, with almost no payment. The workers who dug the Old Cut, many of them prisoners, died in hundreds. The families of Marsh Ashby lost their homes, and some lost their lives, in 1912, because a bank had been left to decay. And the peat, which took about ten thousand years to form, has been mostly used up in about three hundred and fifty.

The drainage also made the fen dependent on machines. Without its pumps, most of Sallow Fen would be under water again within one winter. The board's electricity bill is now more than four million pounds a year. Every person who lives in the basin depends on pumps that most of them have never seen, run by a control room that most of them have never heard of.

What comes next

The Catchment Board published a long-term plan in 2021. It does not propose to give up the dry fen, and it does not propose to drain the remaining wet areas. Instead, it divides the basin into zones. In the lowest zones, where the peat is deepest and the pumping costs are highest, it proposes to help farmers move slowly to wet farming over the next thirty years. In the higher zones, where the peat is gone and the clay is good, it expects normal farming to continue.

The plan also asks for higher banks along the Old Cut to deal with stronger winter rains, and for a new set of tidal gates at Gullmouth by 2040. The cost of the whole plan is estimated at 1.2 billion pounds over fifty years. About half would come from national funds, and the rest from the board's rates and from charges on new building in the basin.

Public meetings on the plan were crowded. Some farmers called the zones the beginning of the end of farming in the fen. Some nature groups said the plan did not go far enough. A few older residents of Marsh Ashby, whose parents had told them about 1912, asked only one question: would the banks hold? The board's answer was careful. The banks will be built for a flood that comes once in two hundred years. A bigger flood is possible, and the plan includes a warning system and safe places on higher ground.

A landscape that people made

If you stand today on the bank of the Old Cut near Hollins Corner, you see a flat land of large fields, straight drains and long roads that run for miles without a bend. It looks natural, in the way that any old landscape looks natural. But almost everything in the view was made by people: the channel under your feet, the level of the water in the ditches, the soil in the fields, even the height of the ground itself.

A short walk to the west takes you to the edge of Kell End Wetland. There the view changes. The ground is uneven, the water is close to the surface, and reed moves in the wind. This looks wild, but it was made by people too, by the closing of drains and the switching off of pumps.

Between these two views is the long argument of Sallow Fen: what land is for, who decides, and who pays. The investors of 1638 had one answer. The Fen Watch had another. The engineers, the drainage boards, the farmers and the nature trusts have each added their own. The water in the basin does not take sides. It goes where the banks, the pumps and the level of the land allow it to go, and it will keep doing that long after the current plan is finished.
