Notes about the hedge on the boundary with number 17

These notes are for me, so that I remember what happened and what I agreed with our neighbour, Mr Petrov. I wrote them on Sunday evening after our talk in the garden.

The hedge between our garden and the garden of number 17 is a row of leylandii trees. They were planted by the people who lived here before us, about fifteen years ago. The hedge is now about six metres high, and it blocks the evening sun from the garden and the kitchen of Mr Petrov. It also drops needles into his pond, and the roots lift the paving on his side.

Mr Petrov first asked about the hedge last summer. I said that I would think about it, and then I did nothing. This summer he asked again, and he was polite but clearly upset. He said that he did not want to complain to the council, but he would if nothing changed.

I looked into the rules. The council has a process for complaints about high hedges. The council can order the owner to cut a hedge down to a set height, often about two metres, and the owner must pay. The council charges a fee for a complaint, so it is better for both of us to agree.

On Sunday we agreed that I will reduce the hedge to a height of two and a half metres, and keep it at that height with a cut twice a year. He agreed that the side of the hedge that faces his garden can be cut from his garden, and that the gardeners can stand on his lawn. We both prefer to keep a hedge, for privacy, and not to replace it with a fence.

I asked two tree surgeons for prices. The first price is 950 pounds, including the removal of all the waste. The second is 780 pounds, but they leave the larger branches for me to take away. A large hedge reduction is best done outside the nesting season, so the work should happen after August.

Mr Petrov offered to pay a quarter of the cost, and I accepted, because the paving on his side needs repair in any case. I will pay the rest, and I will pay for the cuts twice a year after that.

Things to do: book the first tree surgeon for September; tell Mr Petrov the date a week ahead; ask the tree surgeon to check the roots near the pond; write a short note of what we agreed and give a copy to Mr Petrov, so that we both remember it the same way.

I feel better now that we have a plan. He was kind about the delay.
