The last night ferry

For 71 years a small boat carried shift workers across the Elin estuary after midnight. On its final crossing, there were more passengers than seats.

Words by Anneke Duval · Photographs by Sipho Lang · 1 November 2026 · 9 minute read

At ten minutes to one, Ruaridh Tennant unties the rope as he has done about nineteen thousand times before. The engine of the Marsh Harrier is already warm. On a normal night he would carry six or seven people: nurses from the hospital at Southwater, two bakers, a man who cleans the fish market. Tonight he counted fifty-three and then stopped.

The night ferry began in 1955, when the cannery on the north shore started a third shift. The bridge is eleven miles up the river, and no bus goes there after eleven. So the cannery paid a fisherman to take its workers home. The cannery closed in 1989. The ferry did not.

For three decades the district council paid for it, a little less each year. The last contract was for £38,000 a year, which is about £9 for each passenger. In March the council voted to end it. A minibus now makes the trip by the bridge. It takes fifty minutes. The boat takes eight.

"You do not notice a thing like this until the night it is not there."

Dilys Okonjo has taken the boat home from the hospital since 1998. She keeps a list in the back of her diary of the things she has seen from it: a seal with a flatfish, the northern lights twice, a wedding party that had missed the last bus and sang the whole way across. "You do not notice a thing like this until the night it is not there," she says.

Tennant is sixty-eight. He took the job from his uncle in 1987 and has missed, by his own count, four nights: two for storms, one for his daughter's birth, one for a broken shaft. He does not want to talk about the council. He wants to talk about the tide, which tonight runs out so fast that he must point the bow almost upstream to arrive at the south pier.

When the boat touches the pier, no person moves. Then someone starts to clap, and the others join, and Tennant looks at his hands on the wheel. The minibus is waiting at the top of the steps with its lights on. It will leave at one fifteen, as the timetable says.

The Marsh Harrier has been sold to a sailing club further down the coast, which will use it as a safety boat. Tennant says he will go and see it in the spring. He has not decided if he will go by the bridge.
